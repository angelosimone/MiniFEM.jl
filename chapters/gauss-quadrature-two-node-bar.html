15  Gauss–Legendre quadrature for the two-node bar

15.1 Motivation

The preceding chapters developed two separate ideas:

  1. Gauss–Legendre quadrature approximates an integral on \([-1,1]\) by a weighted sum of integrand values;
  2. an affine mapping expresses an integral over a physical interval as an equivalent integral over \([-1,1]\).

These ideas can now be combined to evaluate the stiffness matrix and the consistent distributed-load vector of the two-node linear bar element.

Both element quantities are treated within the same integration framework. Numerical integration is not restricted to the stiffness matrix: whenever an element integral is evaluated numerically, including the consistent load-vector integral, the quadrature rule must be appropriate to its integrand.

15.2 Element relations recalled

Consider a straight two-node bar element extending from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), with local node ordering \(x_1<x_2\) and \(L_e=x_2-x_1>0\).

The affine mapping from the reference element \(\xi\in[-1,1]\) is

\[ x(\xi) = \frac{1-\xi}{2}x_1 + \frac{1+\xi}{2}x_2, \]

and its Jacobian is

\[ J = \frac{dx}{d\xi} = \frac{L_e}{2} >0. \]

Consequently,

\[ dx=J\,d\xi, \qquad \frac{d}{dx} = \frac{1}{J}\frac{d}{d\xi}. \]

The shape-function matrix is

\[ \mathbf{N}(\xi) = \begin{bmatrix} N_1(\xi) & N_2(\xi) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{1-\xi}{2} & \dfrac{1+\xi}{2} \end{bmatrix}, \]

and the strain–displacement matrix is

\[ \mathbf{B} = \frac{1}{J}\frac{d\mathbf{N}}{d\xi} = \begin{bmatrix} -\dfrac{1}{L_e} & \dfrac{1}{L_e} \end{bmatrix}. \]

Both \(\mathbf{N}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are \(1\times2\) row matrices, while \(\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}\) are \(2\times1\) column matrices. For this element, \(J\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are constant, while \(\mathbf{N}\) varies linearly with \(\xi\).

15.3 The common quadrature pattern

Let \(\mathbf{H}(\xi)\) denote a matrix- or vector-valued element integrand. Its integral over the reference interval is approximated by

\[ \boxed{ \int_{-1}^{1}\mathbf{H}(\xi)\,d\xi \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g}w_g\mathbf{H}(\xi_g). } \]

The rule is applied entry by entry. At each Gauss–Legendre point, the complete matrix or vector integrand is evaluated, multiplied by the scalar weight, and added to the accumulated element quantity.

For an integral originally expressed in the physical coordinate, the Jacobian is part of the transformed integrand:

\[ \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\mathbf{G}(x)\,dx = \int_{-1}^{1} \mathbf{G}\!\left(x(\xi)\right)J\,d\xi \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} w_g\mathbf{G}(x_g)J, \qquad x_g=x(\xi_g). \]

The change of coordinate, including \(dx=J\,d\xi\), is exact, and the mapping from \(\xi_g\) to \(x_g\) is also exact. Numerical approximation begins only when the transformed integral is replaced by the finite Gauss–Legendre sum. The factor \(w_g\) belongs to the quadrature rule, whereas \(J\) comes from the coordinate transformation; although they appear as the product \(w_gJ\), the Jacobian is not a quadrature weight.

15.4 Element stiffness matrix

The element stiffness matrix is

\[ \mathbf{K}_e = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}EA\mathbf{B}\,dx. \]

The material property and area may in general depend on position, so we write

\[ E=E(x), \qquad A=A(x). \]

After changing coordinates,

\[ \mathbf{K}_e = \int_{-1}^{1} \mathbf{B}(\xi)^{\mathsf T} E(\xi)A(\xi) \mathbf{B}(\xi)J\,d\xi. \]

Here \(E(\xi)\) and \(A(\xi)\) are shorthand for \(E\!\left(x(\xi)\right)\) and \(A\!\left(x(\xi)\right)\). Consequently, their values at a Gauss–Legendre point are the physical-coordinate values \(E(x_g)\) and \(A(x_g)\), with \(x_g=x(\xi_g)\).

Gauss–Legendre quadrature gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{K}_e \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} w_g \mathbf{B}(\xi_g)^{\mathsf T} E(\xi_g)A(\xi_g) \mathbf{B}(\xi_g)J. } \]

15.4.1 What is actually being integrated?

The compact matrix expression can obscure the fact that ordinary scalar functions are being integrated. Write the strain–displacement matrix as

\[ \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} B_1 & B_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Since \(EA\) is scalar in the axial-bar formulation,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}EA\mathbf{B} &= EA \begin{bmatrix} B_1\\ B_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} B_1 & B_2 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= EA \begin{bmatrix} B_1^2 & B_1B_2\\ B_2B_1 & B_2^2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

The stiffness matrix therefore represents four scalar integrals:

\[ \mathbf{K}_e = \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{1}B_1EA B_1J\,d\xi & \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{1}B_1EA B_2J\,d\xi \\[10pt] \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{1}B_2EA B_1J\,d\xi & \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{1}B_2EA B_2J\,d\xi \end{bmatrix}. \]

For example,

\[ K_{e,12} = \int_{-1}^{1} B_1(\xi)E(\xi)A(\xi)B_2(\xi)J\,d\xi \]

is approximated by

\[ K_{e,12} \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} w_g B_1(\xi_g)E(\xi_g)A(\xi_g)B_2(\xi_g)J. \]

The other three entries are evaluated in exactly the same way. Forming the matrix product at each Gauss–Legendre point simply performs these four scalar calculations together.

15.4.2 Constant properties: one point is sufficient

For the two-node bar,

\[ B_1=-\frac{1}{L_e}, \qquad B_2=\frac{1}{L_e}. \]

If \(E\) and \(A\) are constant within the element,

\[ \mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}EA\mathbf{B} = \frac{EA}{L_e^2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1\\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The Jacobian is also constant, so every scalar entry of the transformed integrand

\[ \mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}EA\mathbf{B}J \]

is constant in \(\xi\) and its polynomial degree is zero. The one-point Gauss–Legendre rule, which integrates polynomials through degree one exactly, is therefore sufficient.

Using

\[ \xi_1=0, \qquad w_1=2, \qquad J=\frac{L_e}{2}, \]

gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{K}_e^{(1)} &= 2 \frac{EA}{L_e^2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1\\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \frac{L_e}{2} \\ &= \boxed{ \frac{EA}{L_e} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1\\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. } \end{aligned} \]

This is the stiffness matrix previously obtained by closed-form integration.

The result is symmetric and has units of force per unit length. Its row sums vanish, and

\[ \mathbf{K}_e \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \]

confirming that a rigid translation produces no axial deformation or internal force.

The two-point rule produces the same exact matrix. Because the integrand is constant and \(w_1+w_2=2\),

\[ \mathbf{K}_e^{(2)} = \mathbf{K}_e^{(1)}. \]

Both rules are exact for this integrand, but the two-point rule obtains the same result at the cost of an additional integrand evaluation.

15.4.3 Varying properties

If \(E\) or \(A\) varies within the element, the degree of each stiffness integrand is determined by the product

\[ B_i(\xi)E(\xi)A(\xi)B_j(\xi)J. \]

For the present affine two-node element, \(B_i\), \(B_j\), and \(J\) are constant. The variation therefore comes from \(E(\xi)A(\xi)\). If this product is polynomial, its degree determines the minimum exact Gauss–Legendre rule. If it is not polynomial, the quadrature result is generally approximate and its convergence must be assessed.

15.5 Consistent distributed-load vector

Let \(q(x)\) denote the distributed axial force per unit length, taken as positive when it acts in the positive \(x\) direction. Thus, \(q\) has units of force per unit length. The corresponding consistent element load vector, whose entries are equivalent nodal forces, is

\[ \mathbf{f}_e^q = \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T}q(x)\,dx. \]

Writing

\[ q(\xi) = q\!\left(x(\xi)\right), \]

the exact coordinate transformation gives

\[ \mathbf{f}_e^q = \int_{-1}^{1} \mathbf{N}(\xi)^{\mathsf T}q(\xi)J\,d\xi. \]

Gauss–Legendre quadrature then gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{f}_e^q \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} w_g \mathbf{N}(\xi_g)^{\mathsf T} q(\xi_g)J. } \]

At each Gauss–Legendre point, \(q(\xi_g)=q(x_g)\), where \(x_g=x(\xi_g)\). This is the same weighted-sum structure used for the stiffness matrix.

15.5.1 What is actually being integrated?

Since

\[ \mathbf{N}^{\mathsf T} = \begin{bmatrix} N_1\\ N_2 \end{bmatrix}, \]

the vector expression contains two scalar integrals:

\[ \mathbf{f}_e^q = \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{1}N_1(\xi)q(\xi)J\,d\xi \\[10pt] \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{1}N_2(\xi)q(\xi)J\,d\xi \end{bmatrix}. \]

For example,

\[ f_{e,1}^q = \int_{-1}^{1}N_1(\xi)q(\xi)J\,d\xi \]

is approximated by

\[ f_{e,1}^q \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} w_gN_1(\xi_g)q(\xi_g)J. \]

Again, the vector notation simply collects the two scalar integrals into a single compact expression; integration is understood componentwise.

15.6 Uniform distributed load

Suppose

\[ q(\xi)=q \]

is constant. The Jacobian is constant, while each shape function is linear in \(\xi\). Each scalar integrand

\[ N_i(\xi)qJ \]

therefore has polynomial degree one. The one-point rule is exact.

At \(\xi_1=0\),

\[ N_1(0)=N_2(0)=\frac12. \]

Using \(w_1=2\) and \(J=L_e/2\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_e^{q,(1)} &= 2 \begin{bmatrix} N_1(0)\\ N_2(0) \end{bmatrix} q\frac{L_e}{2} \\ &= 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1/2\\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix} q\frac{L_e}{2} \\ &= \boxed{ \frac{qL_e}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. } \end{aligned} \]

This is the consistent distributed-load vector previously obtained by closed-form integration. The two-point rule also gives the same exact result, but one point is sufficient.

The sum of the two entries is

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{f}_e^q = qL_e, \]

which is the total distributed force acting on the element.

15.7 Linearly varying distributed load

Suppose the distributed-load intensity varies linearly between

\[ q_1=q(x_1) \]

and

\[ q_2=q(x_2). \]

The load intensity can be interpolated as

\[ q(\xi) = N_1(\xi)q_1 + N_2(\xi)q_2. \]

Here \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are distributed-load intensities evaluated at the element nodes. They are not concentrated nodal forces.

Both \(N_i(\xi)\) and \(q(\xi)\) are linear. Their product is quadratic, while \(J\) is constant. Each entry of

\[ \mathbf{N}(\xi)^{\mathsf T}q(\xi)J \]

therefore has degree two. A one-point rule is not generally exact, whereas a two-point rule is exact.

15.7.1 Result from the one-point rule

The one-point Gauss–Legendre rule has the quadrature point \(\xi_1=0\) and weight \(w_1=2\). At this point,

\[ \mathbf{N}(0)^{\mathsf T} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2\\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad q(0) = \frac{q_1+q_2}{2}, \qquad J = \frac{L_e}{2}. \]

The resulting approximation is therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_e^{q,(1)} &= w_1 \mathbf{N}(0)^{\mathsf T} q(0) J \\[1mm] &= 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1/2\\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix} \frac{q_1+q_2}{2} \frac{L_e}{2} \\[1mm] &= \boxed{ \frac{L_e}{4} \begin{bmatrix} q_1+q_2\\ q_1+q_2 \end{bmatrix} }. \end{aligned} \]

Because it samples the integrand only at the midpoint, the one-point rule assigns the same force to both nodes, regardless of the direction of the load variation. Its entries nevertheless sum to

\[ \frac{L_e}{2}(q_1+q_2), \]

which is the correct total force under a linearly varying load. This does not make the consistent load vector exact: the individual load-vector integrands \(N_i(\xi)q(\xi)J\) are quadratic and are therefore underintegrated by the one-point rule. When the two entries are summed, however, the partition-of-unity property \(N_1+N_2=1\) reduces the aggregate integrand to \(q(\xi)J\). Because \(J\) is constant, this integrand is linear and the one-point rule integrates it exactly. The rule therefore recovers the total force but not its consistent nodal distribution.

15.7.2 Result from the two-point rule

The two-point Gauss–Legendre rule uses

\[ \xi_1=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \qquad \xi_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \qquad w_1=w_2=1. \]

At these two quadrature points, the shape-function vectors are

\[ \mathbf{N}(\xi_1)^{\mathsf T} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1+1/\sqrt{3}\\ 1-1/\sqrt{3} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{N}(\xi_2)^{\mathsf T} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1-1/\sqrt{3}\\ 1+1/\sqrt{3} \end{bmatrix}. \]

The corresponding values of the interpolated load are

\[ \begin{aligned} q(\xi_1) &= \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)q_1 + \left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)q_2 \right], \\ q(\xi_2) &= \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)q_1 + \left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)q_2 \right]. \end{aligned} \]

The quadrature sum is

\[ \mathbf{f}_e^{q,(2)} = \sum_{g=1}^{2} w_g \mathbf{N}(\xi_g)^{\mathsf T} q(\xi_g) J, \]

which gives, with \(J=L_e/2\),

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{f}_e^{q,(2)} = \frac{L_e}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 2q_1+q_2\\ q_1+2q_2 \end{bmatrix}. } \]

Each component of the load-vector integrand, \(N_i(\xi)q(\xi)J\), is quadratic because both \(N_i(\xi)\) and \(q(\xi)\) are linear, while \(J\) is constant. The two-point Gauss–Legendre rule is exact for polynomials of degree up to three and therefore evaluates both components exactly. Unlike the one-point result, the two entries distinguish between \(q_1\) and \(q_2\): the node nearer the larger endpoint load receives the larger equivalent nodal force. Their sum remains

\[ \frac{L_e}{2}(q_1+q_2), \]

so the two-point rule recovers both the correct total force and its consistent nodal distribution.

Direct analytical integration provides an independent verification. Since

\[ \int_{-1}^{1}N_1^2\,d\xi = \int_{-1}^{1}N_2^2\,d\xi = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad \int_{-1}^{1}N_1N_2\,d\xi = \frac{1}{3}, \]

the exact integral gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_e^q &= \frac{L_e}{2} \begin{bmatrix} \displaystyle q_1\int_{-1}^{1}N_1^2\,d\xi + q_2\int_{-1}^{1}N_1N_2\,d\xi \\[8pt] \displaystyle q_1\int_{-1}^{1}N_1N_2\,d\xi + q_2\int_{-1}^{1}N_2^2\,d\xi \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \frac{L_e}{6} \begin{bmatrix} 2q_1+q_2\\ q_1+2q_2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

The two-point Gauss–Legendre rule therefore agrees with direct integration. The sum of the equivalent nodal forces is

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{f}_e^q = \frac{L_e}{2}(q_1+q_2), \]

which is the exact resultant of the linearly varying distributed load. If \(q_1=q_2=q\), the vector reduces to

\[ \mathbf{f}_e^q = \frac{qL_e}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

as required.

15.8 Choosing the rule from the integrand

Although the stiffness matrix and load vector are written in matrix and vector form, quadrature is applied to their individual scalar entries. The minimum exact rule is therefore determined from the polynomial degree of each entry of the complete transformed integrand.

Element quantity Scalar integrand Degree in \(\xi\) Minimum exact rule
stiffness, constant \(EA\) \(B_iEA B_jJ\) 0 one point
load vector, uniform \(q\) \(N_iqJ\) 1 one point
load vector, linear \(q\) \(N_iqJ\) 2 two points

The quadrature rule must consequently be selected only after all factors in the transformed integrand have been identified. Considering the load intensity alone can lead to the wrong choice. For example, a linearly varying \(q(\xi)\) is a polynomial of degree one, but each entry of the consistent load-vector integrand also contains a linear shape function \(N_i(\xi)\). Because \(J\) is constant for the present affine mapping, the complete product \(N_i(\xi)q(\xi)J\) is quadratic and requires a two-point rule for exact integration.

15.8.1 One framework does not imply one minimum rule

Using the same quadrature framework for stiffness and loading does not mean that their integrands always have the same minimum exact rule. Each integrand has its own degree.

If stiffness and load contributions are accumulated in one element loop, a single rule may be selected that is sufficient for the highest degree present. For constant \(EA\) and a linearly varying distributed load, a two-point rule is therefore sufficient for both quantities:

  • it is exact, although more than necessary, for the constant stiffness integrand;
  • it is the minimum exact rule for the quadratic load-vector integrands.

This preserves a common element integration procedure without integrating the load vector inadequately.

15.9 Exactness and approximation in an element calculation

The rule

\[ p\leq2n_g-1 \]

applies to the complete scalar integrand expressed in the reference coordinate \(\xi\). For the present two-node bar, the affine mapping makes \(J\) constant and \(\mathbf{B}\) constant, so the degrees are easy to identify.

If material properties or loading are non-polynomial functions of \(\xi\), no finite-degree exactness guarantee follows. The quadrature result is then generally approximate. Increasing the number of Gauss–Legendre points and examining the change in the element matrix, element vector, or final finite element response provides a practical convergence check.

The degree counts in the table rely on the affine mapping of the two-node bar, for which both \(J\) and its inverse are constant. In more general element mappings, these quantities may vary with position and must also be included when determining the degree of the complete integrand. Those cases will be considered when the corresponding element formulations are introduced.

15.10 The element-level mathematical procedure

After the exact transformation to the reference coordinate, the element stiffness matrix and consistent distributed-load vector have the forms

\[ \mathbf{K}_e = \int_{-1}^{1} \mathbf{B}(\xi)^{\mathsf T} E\!\left(x(\xi)\right) A\!\left(x(\xi)\right) \mathbf{B}(\xi) J\,d\xi \]

and

\[ \mathbf{f}_e^q = \int_{-1}^{1} \mathbf{N}(\xi)^{\mathsf T} q(\xi) J\,d\xi. \]

For the present affine two-node bar, \(J=L_e/2\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are constant. They remain visible in the complete transformed integrands because they contribute to the element quantities and must be accounted for when selecting the quadrature rule.

The quadrature order is determined from the complete scalar entries of these transformed integrands. If their highest polynomial degrees are \(d_K\) and \(d_q\), respectively, the minimum numbers of Gauss–Legendre points required for exact integration are

\[ n_K = \left\lceil\frac{d_K+1}{2}\right\rceil, \qquad n_q = \left\lceil\frac{d_q+1}{2}\right\rceil, \]

because an \(n_g\)-point Gauss–Legendre rule is exact for polynomials of degree up to \(2n_g-1\). If the stiffness matrix and load vector are accumulated using the same quadrature rule, the number of points must satisfy

\[ n_g = \max(n_K,n_q). \]

For a selected rule with quadrature points \(\xi_g\) and weights \(w_g\), define

\[ x_g=x(\xi_g), \qquad \mathbf{N}_g=\mathbf{N}(\xi_g), \qquad \mathbf{B}_g=\mathbf{B}(\xi_g), \]

together with

\[ E_g=E(x_g), \qquad A_g=A(x_g), \qquad q_g=q(x_g)=q(\xi_g). \]

The element quantities are then evaluated as

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{K}_e \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} w_g \mathbf{B}_g^{\mathsf T} E_gA_g \mathbf{B}_g J } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf{f}_e^q \approx \sum_{g=1}^{n_g} w_g \mathbf{N}_g^{\mathsf T} q_g J. } \]

Each approximation becomes an equality when the selected rule integrates every scalar entry of the corresponding transformed integrand exactly. Once \(\mathbf{K}_e\) and \(\mathbf{f}_e^q\) have been evaluated, they are assembled using the local-to-global operations established in the preceding MiniFEM chapters.

15.11 Verification against the closed-form element

The closed-form element results derived earlier provide reference values for the quadrature calculations. For constant \(EA\), the exact stiffness matrix is

\[ \mathbf{K}_e = \frac{EA}{L_e} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1\\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

while, for a uniform distributed load \(q\), the exact consistent load vector is

\[ \mathbf{f}_e^q = \frac{qL_e}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

One-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature reproduces both closed-form results exactly. This agreement checks the combined use of the coordinate mapping, Jacobian, transformed integrands, quadrature point, weight, and accumulation. Its validity is limited to these particular integrands, whose degrees do not exceed one.

The linearly varying load provides the complementary check. Its quadratic load-vector integrands are not integrated exactly by the one-point rule, even though that rule recovers their correct aggregate force. The two-point rule reproduces the closed-form consistent load vector and therefore recovers both the total force and its correct nodal distribution.

15.12 Exercises

  1. Expand every entry of \(\mathbf{B}^{\mathsf T}EA\mathbf{B}J\) for constant \(EA\) and verify its polynomial degree in \(\xi\).
  2. Evaluate the constant-\(EA\) stiffness matrix using the two-point rule and verify explicitly that it agrees with the one-point and closed-form results.
  3. For a uniform distributed load, evaluate both scalar entries of the consistent load vector separately using one-point quadrature.
  4. Let \(q_1=0\) and \(q_2=q_0\). Compare the one- and two-point load vectors and verify that both have the correct total force while only the two-point vector is consistent with exact integration.
  5. Suppose \(EA\) varies linearly with \(\xi\). Determine the minimum Gauss–Legendre rule required for exact stiffness integration for the present affine two-node element.
  6. Suppose \(EA\) is constant and \(q(\xi)\) is quadratic. Determine the degree of each consistent load-vector integrand and the minimum exact Gauss–Legendre rule.
ImportantKey points to remember

  • Rewriting an element integral from \(x\) to \(\xi\), transforming \(dx=J\,d\xi\), transforming derivatives, and mapping \(\xi_g\) to \(x_g\) are exact operations. Numerical approximation begins only when the transformed integral is replaced by a finite Gauss–Legendre sum.

  • The quadrature weight \(w_g\) belongs to the numerical integration rule, whereas the Jacobian \(J\) belongs to the coordinate transformation. Their product \(w_gJ\) combines two factors with different origins.

  • Matrix and vector quadrature is ordinary scalar quadrature applied entry by entry. At each Gauss–Legendre point, the complete matrix or vector integrand is formed and its weighted contribution is accumulated.

  • The quadrature order must be selected from the degree of the complete transformed scalar integrand. A rule with \(n_g\) points integrates every polynomial of degree \(p\leq2n_g-1\) exactly.

  • For a straight two-node bar with constant \(EA\), the stiffness integrands have degree zero, so one-point quadrature is exact.

  • For a uniform distributed load, the consistent load-vector integrands have degree one, so one-point quadrature is exact. For a linearly varying load, multiplication by the linear shape functions produces quadratic integrands, so two-point quadrature is required for exact integration.

  • Using more points than necessary can still give an exact result, but requires additional integrand evaluations. Underintegration may reproduce an aggregate quantity, such as the total distributed force, without reproducing the correct element vector.
